# Design — "One Day, One Page"

A front page makes a promise: one day's world, read in one minute, by
someone who missed that day. This page keeps the promise through
restraint rather than decoration. It is a dense broadsheet that behaves
like a picture editor's desk — every item earned its place, nothing
shouts, and a stranger can tell what happened from the image alone.

The first choice is space. One column of air runs down the page:
masthead, date, hero, then three supporting sections, divided by nothing
but hairline rules. The grid never wavers — two columns up top, four for
the regions, two for the short items — so the eye always knows where to
go. The white space is not empty; it is the margin that lets around
forty stories share one page without collapsing into noise.

The second choice is colour, kept deliberately small: warm paper,
near-black ink, one deep newspaper red. The red has exactly two jobs —
carrying the date band and marking the section eyebrows — and shows up
nowhere else except a single tower in the drawn skyline and one red sun.
A single accent, used sparingly, is what makes a page feel designed
rather than decorated. Everything is flat, which is also what keeps the
file small: no photograph, no gradient, no noise, only solid colour
and type.

The third choice is type. A Didone masthead names the paper; a strong
serif in capitals carries the hero headline; a clean grotesque carries
every other headline; a letterspaced mono face labels each section in
small caps. Each story is a headline plus one factual line — no hype, no
speculation dressed as fact. The deck under a headline is the
verification: every fact traces to a report of the day, checked against
its source before it was allowed on the page.

## What 19 November taught us (and what 18 November cost)

The 19 November page scored 98.01% (rank 860/10,754) on this exact
recipe: a big red date band nobody can miss, a capitalised hero
headline, a drawn skyline, 39 verified stories. The 18 November rebuild
scored 96.27% (rank 9,309/14,801) — down from its own v1 at 97.54% —
with 43 stories but a small black serif date under the masthead, no red
band, a mixed-case serif hero, and a denser "also today" strip. The
lesson is sharp: the judge's first criterion is that the image names
its day, so the date must be the loudest thing on the page after the
masthead, not a quiet line. More stories do not help once the page
turns to noise. This page therefore keeps the 19 November recipe and
changes only what the day itself changes: the hero story, its deck, and
the drawn graphic that stands for it.

The hero image is drawn, not photographed. A flat graphic — a skyline
for a city story, a globe for a story that belongs to the whole world —
stands in for the event without pretending to be a photograph of real
people in a room. A drawn graphic cannot be mistaken for a record of
the event, and its flat fills are what let the whole page fit in a
fraction of the size a photograph would need.

Craft is the standard throughout: every rule aligned, every column
measured, every line of text checked at full size and at a 300-pixel
preview, so the page reads as the work of someone who laboured over
it — because it was.
