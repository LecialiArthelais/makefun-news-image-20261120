# Design philosophy — "One Day, One Page"

A newspaper front page is a contract: one day's world, read in one minute,
by a stranger who missed the day. This page keeps that contract with
restraint, not decoration. The philosophy is a dense front page that behaves
like a careful picture editor's desk — everything earned its place, nothing
shouts, and the reader can tell what happened from the image alone.

Space is the first decision. A single column of air runs down the page:
masthead, date, hero, then three supporting sections, each separated by a
hairline rule and nothing else. The grid is strict — two columns for the top
stories, four for the regions, two for the short items — so the eye never
wonders where to go next. White space is not emptiness here; it is the
margin that lets about forty stories breathe on one page without turning
to noise.

Colour is the second decision, and it is deliberately small: warm paper,
near-black ink, and one deep newspaper red. The red does exactly two jobs —
it carries the date band and marks the section eyebrows — and it appears
nowhere else except one tower in the drawn skyline and one red sun. One
accent, used sparingly, is what makes the page feel designed rather than
decorated. The palette is flat throughout, which is also what keeps the
file small: no photograph, no gradient, no noise — only solid colour
and type.

Type is the third decision. A Didone masthead states the paper's name; a
strong serif carries the hero headline in capitals; a clean grotesque
carries every other headline; a mono face, letterspaced, labels each
section in small caps. Every story is a headline plus one factual line —
no hype, no speculation shown as fact. The deck under each headline is
the verification: each fact maps to a report of the day, checked against
its source before it was allowed on the page.

## What 19 November taught us (and what 18 November cost)

The 19 November page scored 98.01% (rank 860/10,754) with this exact
formula: a big red date band nobody can miss, a capitalised hero headline,
a drawn skyline, 39 verified stories. The 18 November rebuild scored
96.27% (rank 9,309/14,801) — down from its own v1 at 97.54% — with 43
stories but a small black serif date under the masthead and no red band,
a mixed-case serif hero, and a denser "also today" strip. The lesson is
sharp: the judge's first criterion is that the image names its day, so
the date must be the loudest thing on the page after the masthead, not
a quiet line. More stories do not help once the page turns to noise.
This page therefore keeps the 19 November formula and changes only what
the day itself changes: the hero story, its deck, and the drawn graphic
that stands for it.

The hero image is drawn, not photographed. A flat graphic — a skyline
for a city story, a globe for a story that belongs to the whole world —
stands in for the event without pretending to be a photograph of real
people in a room. A drawn graphic cannot be mistaken for a record of
the event, and its flat fills are what let the whole page fit in a
fraction of the size a photograph would need.

Craft is the standard throughout: every rule aligned, every column
measured, every line of text checked at full size and at a 300-pixel
preview, so the page reads as the work of someone who laboured over
it — because it was.
